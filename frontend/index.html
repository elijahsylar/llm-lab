FINE-TUNING // FIELD GUIDE
A plain-English guide to training your own LLMs on this machine.
01 — What is Fine-Tuning?

A large language model (LLM) is pre-trained on hundreds of billions of words from the internet, books, and code. That gives it broad general knowledge — it can write, reason, code, and answer questions — but it's not specialized for anything in particular.

Fine-tuning is the process of taking that pre-trained model and teaching it to behave differently by showing it examples of exactly what you want.

ANALOGY
Think of the base model as a smart new hire with a great education. Fine-tuning is their on-the-job training. You don't re-teach them everything — you just show them the specifics of your operation until they get it right.

Examples of what you can do with fine-tuning:

  • Teach a model your writing style or tone
  • Train it on domain-specific knowledge (medical, legal, code)
  • Make it always respond in a certain format (JSON, bullet points)
  • Create a character or persona it consistently maintains
  • Improve performance on a specific narrow task
02 — Why QLoRA? (The VRAM Problem)

A 7-8 billion parameter model stores its weights as floating-point numbers. Full-precision, that's about 28GB of VRAM. Training it normally requires even more — usually 40-80GB. Your RTX 4060 has 8GB.

QLoRA solves this with two tricks stacked together:

1
Quantization (the "Q")
Compress the base model's weights from 32-bit floats down to 4-bit integers. This shrinks a 7B model from ~28GB to ~4-5GB — small enough to fit in your GPU. There's a tiny quality loss, but it's barely noticeable in practice.
2
LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation
Instead of rewriting the entire model (impossible at 4-bit), LoRA adds small extra adapter layers on top of the frozen base. Only these adapters get trained. They're tiny — usually 50-200MB versus 4GB for the base model. After training, you save just the adapter.
Result: Fine-tune a 7-8B model in ~5-6GB VRAM on your RTX 4060. Without QLoRA, you'd need an A100 (80GB) in a data center.
03 — Your Dataset

The dataset is the most important part. The model will imitate exactly what it sees, so your data quality directly determines output quality — "garbage in, garbage out" applies completely here.

This system uses a simple instruction / output format. Each training example is one line of JSON:

{"instruction": "Write a haiku about neural networks.",
 "output": "Weights shift like tides /\nGradients flow through the dark /\nLoss finds its bottom"}

Save as a .jsonl file (one JSON object per line) and drop it into finetune/datasets/.

DO
  • 100–1000 high-quality examples for specialization
  • Consistent style and format across all examples
  • Diverse coverage of the task you care about
  • Proofread outputs — errors get learned too
AVOID
  • Duplicate or near-duplicate examples
  • Mixed writing styles (unless intentional)
  • Very long outputs with 2048 token limit — they get cut off
  • Fewer than ~50 examples — not enough signal
04 — What Happens During Training
1
Load — The base model loads in 4-bit. LoRA adapters attach on top. Only adapter weights are marked as trainable.
2
Forward pass — A batch of examples gets fed in. The model predicts the output tokens one by one.
3
Loss calculation — The predicted tokens are compared to the real outputs. The difference is called loss. High loss = wrong. Low loss = right.
4
Backward pass — The loss is used to calculate how each adapter weight contributed to the error (this is the "gradient"). Weights that made things worse get nudged in the right direction.
5
Repeat — This runs for every batch, for every epoch. The loss curve (visible in the Finetune Lab tab) shows the model getting smarter over time.
6
Save — The adapter weights are saved to finetune/exports/. The base model is unchanged.
05 — Parameter Reference

These are the knobs in the Finetune Lab. Here's what they actually do:

TRAINING PARAMETERS
Epochs
default: 3
How many times the model sees your entire dataset. More epochs = more learning, but past a point the model starts overfitting (memorizing instead of generalizing). For small datasets (<500 examples), 3-5 is typical. Watch the loss curve — if it stops dropping, stop training.
Batch Size
default: 4
How many training examples to process at once before updating the weights. Larger batches = more stable gradient estimates, but more VRAM. On your 8GB GPU, 4 is safe for most 7-8B models. Going to 8 might work if your examples are short.
Gradient Accumulation
default: 4
A trick to simulate a larger batch without using more VRAM. Instead of updating after every batch, it accumulates the gradients across N steps, then updates. Effective batch = Batch Size × Grad Accum. Default gives you an effective batch of 16, which is a good target.
Learning Rate
default: 2e-4
How big a step to take when adjusting weights after each update. Think of it as the size of the nudge. Too high: training is unstable, loss bounces around. Too low: training barely does anything. 2e-4 (that's 0.0002) is a well-tested default for QLoRA. If loss is chaotic, try 1e-4.
Max Length
default: 2048
Maximum number of tokens per training example. Examples longer than this get cut off. A token is roughly ¾ of a word — 2048 tokens ≈ 1500 words. Reduce to 1024 if you're running out of VRAM during training.
LORA PARAMETERS
Rank (r)
default: 16
The size of the LoRA adapter matrices. Higher rank = the adapter can capture more complex patterns = more capacity to learn, but also more VRAM and slower training. r=16 is the sweet spot for most tasks. Use r=32 or r=64 if the task is complex and you're not memory-constrained.
Alpha
default: 32
A scaling factor applied to the LoRA updates. The effective learning scale is alpha/r. The convention is to set alpha = 2× rank (so r=16 → alpha=32). You can leave this alone unless you have a specific reason to change it.
Dropout
default: 0.05
During training, randomly "turns off" 5% of adapter connections on each pass. This prevents overfitting by stopping the model from relying too heavily on any single path. 0.05 is a gentle regularization — you probably won't need to change this.
06 — Reading the Loss Curve

The loss curve is the heartbeat monitor of your training run. Loss measures how wrong the model's predictions are — lower is better, and you want it to trend downward.

Starting loss 2.0 – 4.0
Normal. The model hasn't learned your data yet.
Loss drops quickly in epoch 1
Great — the model is picking up the pattern in your data.
Loss levels off
The model has learned what it can from your data. Stop here or let it run to the end of your epoch count — not much benefit after this.
Loss rises after dropping
Overfitting — the model is memorizing your training examples instead of generalizing. Use fewer epochs, or add more diverse training data.
Loss stays high and barely moves
Something is wrong. Common causes: learning rate too low, data format issues, or the base model isn't a good fit for the task.
Loss explodes (NaN or >10)
Learning rate is too high. Lower it by 10× and restart.
07 — After Training: Getting Your Model
1
Adapter saved — Training produces a small adapter folder in finetune/exports/your-name/. This is ~50-200MB and contains only the LoRA weights, not the full model.
2
Merge & export to GGUF — Click "→ GGUF" in the completed runs list. It will show you the command to run export_gguf.py, which merges the adapter back into the base model and converts it to GGUF format.
3
GGUF appears in the model list — The exported file lands in models/shared/ (or $LLM_LAB_SHARED_MODELS_DIR). The registry rescans on refresh, so it shows up in the Chat sidebar without a restart.
4
Test it — Select your fine-tuned model in the Chat tab and see how it behaves. Compare it to the base model on the same prompts.
08 — Glossary
Token A word or word-piece. "Fine-tuning" = 3 tokens. Most models have a limit of 4096–128k tokens per context. Your VRAM limits how many you can use for training.
VRAM GPU memory. Your RTX 4060 has 8GB. Every model, batch, and gradient lives here during training. Running out = crash.
Parameters The numbers inside a model that encode its knowledge. A "7B model" has 7 billion of these numbers.
Quantization Compressing weights from 32-bit floats to 4-bit integers. Like reducing a photo from RAW to JPEG — smaller, tiny quality loss, still very usable.
LoRA Low-Rank Adaptation. Adds small trainable matrices alongside frozen model weights instead of retraining everything.
QLoRA LoRA applied to a 4-bit quantized base model. Enables fine-tuning on consumer GPUs.
Adapter The LoRA weights that come out of training. Tiny file, stacks on top of any copy of the base model.
GGUF A file format for running models locally (used by llama.cpp and this system). After merging your adapter into the base model, you export to GGUF to use it in the Chat tab.
Loss A number measuring how wrong the model's predictions are. You want this to go down during training.
Epoch One full pass through your entire training dataset.
Gradient The direction and size of the weight adjustment needed to reduce loss. Calculated automatically via backpropagation.
Overfitting When the model memorizes your training data instead of learning the underlying pattern. Signs: loss goes up late in training, model outputs near-exact copies of training examples.
Inference Using the model to generate output — what happens in the Chat tab. As opposed to training.
Base model The pre-trained foundation (e.g. Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen). You're not changing this — you're adding a LoRA adapter on top of it.
